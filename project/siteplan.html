01 — Origins

A brief history of skateboarding

Skateboarding started as a way to surf when the ocean was flat, and it has been reinventing itself every decade since. Here's how a wooden crate on roller-skate wheels turned into an Olympic sport.

  1. 1950s

    Sidewalk surfing

    California surfers looking for something to do between swells nailed roller-skate wheels to wooden boards and took to the sidewalks. It had no name yet, and no rules — just surfing's footwork translated to pavement.

  2. 1970s

    Urethane and the Z-Boys

    Urethane wheels replaced clay and metal, finally giving skaters real grip. In drought-emptied Los Angeles backyards, the Zephyr team — the Z-Boys — poured their surf style into empty swimming pools and invented vert skating almost by accident.

  3. 1980s

    The vert boom, then the street pivot

    Skateparks rose and fell with insurance costs, so skaters took the ramp tricks they'd learned back to the streets. The ollie — a no-hands jump — became the technical seed from which nearly every modern street trick would grow.

  4. 1990s

    Street skating goes technical

    Curbs, stairs, and handrails became the new terrain. Flip tricks multiplied, video parts replaced contest wins as the measure of skill, and skate videos built a DIY media culture of their own.

  5. 2000s

    Mainstream and the X Games

    Televised competitions and video games introduced skateboarding to people who had never touched a board, turning a subculture into a global industry without fully sanding down its edges.

  6. 2020s

    The Olympic stage

    Skateboarding debuted at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics, putting street and park skating in front of the widest audience the sport has ever had — a long way from a plank with roller-skate wheels.

02 — Icons

Skaters who shaped the sport

A handful of names come up in nearly every conversation about how skateboarding got here.

Rodney Mullen

The godfather of flat-ground tricks

Widely credited with inventing the flatground ollie, the kickflip, and dozens of other flip tricks that are now considered the basic vocabulary of street skating.

Tony Hawk

Vert skating's most recognizable face

Landed the first documented 900 in competition and helped carry skateboarding into mainstream pop culture through contests, media, and a namesake video game series.

Nyjah Huston

Modern street skating's dominant competitor

One of the most decorated street skaters of his generation, known for technical precision on rails and stairs at a level few have matched.

Sky Brown

Olympic-era park skating

Became one of the youngest Olympic medalists in skateboarding history, representing the sport's newest generation on its biggest stage.

03 — Fundamentals

Tricks every skater learns

Most street tricks are variations built on top of a small set of fundamentals.

  • Ollie

    Beginner

    A no-hands jump made by snapping the tail down and sliding the front foot up the board. The foundation for nearly every other trick.

  • Kickflip

    Intermediate

    An ollie combined with a flick off the edge of the board that flips it once beneath the rider's feet before it's caught and landed.

  • 50-50 Grind

    Intermediate

    Both trucks lock onto a curb, rail, or ledge and slide across it evenly, one of the first grinds most skaters learn.

  • Manual

    Beginner

    Balancing on the back two wheels while rolling, essentially a skateboard's version of a wheelie. Often linked between other tricks.

  • 360 Flip

    Advanced

    A kickflip and a 360-degree shove-it combined into a single rotation, considered one of the harder tricks to land consistently.
